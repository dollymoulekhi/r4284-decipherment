DECODE R4284 / R4325

Reading a Swedish–Latin cipher letter

An alphabet preserved in another archival record turns parts of R4284’s numbers into words. Here is what the candidate match reveals—and what it does not.

25 October 2026 · By Dolly Moulekhi · Preliminary research note

Among the numbers in this historical letter is a phrase that can be read as nobis et patriae securum: “safe for us and our country.” It emerges when the numbers are replaced with letters using an alphabet strip in a separate key record, R4325. Other passages yield Swedish instructions and references to heirs, friends, and relatives.

This is a candidate archival-key match, not a complete translation. The useful result is a set of readable passages within a still-fragmentary letter. The examples below separate what the program produces from the editorial work needed to understand it.

Partial reading · Not independently verified · 96.68% coverage—not accuracy.

The candidate alphabet assigns letters to 729 of 754 numeric occurrences. This does not mean that 96.68% of the message is correctly deciphered or understood. Readable passages coexist with unresolved groups and anomalous letter sequences.

Independent transcription, specialist language review, and a complete translation are still pending. This research note has not been peer reviewed. No endorsement by the archive or database is claimed.

One worked example

From numbers to “safe for us and our country”

The proposed key is the horizontal alphabet strip below the first square grid on I25940 in R4325. It gives several numbers for each letter. For example, 65 gives n, 40 gives o, 28 gives b, 2 gives i, and 19 gives s.

  2. The program’s literal output

    nobisetpatriaesecurum

    The program substitutes letters. It does not supply spaces or translate the phrase.

  3. The editorial reading

    nobis et patriae securum

    Spaces are added by a reader; the letters are unchanged.

  4. The provisional English meaning

    “Safe for us and our country.”

    This is a phrase within an incomplete sentence, not a translation of the entire letter.

Why not print a complete English letter?

The mechanical output still contains anomalous words and unread groups. A fluent reconstruction would risk inventing the missing connections. The full inherited transcription and derived line-by-line edition are also withheld from this release while reuse terms are clarified. These excerpts are not a substitute for that full evidence.

A cautious interpretation

What does the letter seem to be about?

The readable fragments suggest instructions or negotiations involving Baltic ports, with concern for the safety of “us and our country.” They also mention heirs, friends, and relatives. That makes a diplomatic context plausible.

This is a tentative thematic summary, not a recovered historical narrative. We cannot yet identify the negotiating parties, the proposed settlement, or the role of the relatives. Unread groups may contain precisely the names and terms needed to answer those questions.

The result therefore advances the letter from an attempted reading to a testable candidate match with readable excerpts. It does not establish that every mapped letter is correct or that the whole document has been understood.

Measure the result

How much has actually been decoded?

The numerical letter in DECODE R4284 is held at Riksarkivet, Sweden, under Chifferklaver låda II:114. Applying the horizontal alphabet strip on image I25940 in R4325, Chifferklaver låda II:152, produces sequences readable as historical Swedish interspersed with Latin.

This is a proposed correspondence between the alphabet in a surviving key compilation and the letter. It does not establish that this exact sheet was used by the writer, or that the compilation's word codes apply to the letter.

729 / 754

Numeric occurrences with alphabet assignments in the reviewed transcription.
96.68% coverage. Accuracy is not measured.

The remaining numeric occurrences are 23 unmarked groups above 108 and two uncertain readings. The 25 spans already written in clear script are counted separately. A repeated line is retained in the literal edition; excluding it gives 707 assignments out of 732 numeric occurrences.

The public R4284 record gives a broad seventeenth-century date range. A narrower 1628/Camerarius association comes from earlier work on a manuscript annotation and needs archival confirmation. R4325 is catalogued from 1640. The alphabet match does not resolve that dating question.

Method and credit

Apply an archived key; preserve what does not fit.

This investigation builds on Daniel Bourdeau's earlier R4284 attempt, including its transcription and notes. That attempt tested Latin-scored homophonic searches and two archival keys, without obtaining a reading. It is not work newly performed or discovered here.

Further trials examined other archived alphabets. The promising match is specifically R4325's horizontal strip below its first square grid—not that grid. Its transcription contains 104 distinct assignments in the range 1–108. Four values, 22, 51, 74 and 89, are unassigned. Y and Z share a value set, so the literal output preserves (y/z).

  1. Keep the earlier transcription and the image-reviewed version separate.
  2. Apply the fixed alphabet to each unambiguous numeric token.
  3. Preserve clear-script words, uncertain digits and unresolved groups.
  4. Explore exact digit partitions separately, without inserting them into the baseline.
  5. Record both readable excerpts and unresolved readings.

AI assisted analysis, coding, and drafting. The decoder applies explicit rules; it does not call an AI model to invent or regenerate plaintext. An AI-suggested reading is not independent historical evidence. A complete model-by-model chronology has not yet been reconstructed.

Where the evidence stops

The unresolved text stays visible.

All 23 unmarked large-group occurrences remain unresolved. Six have exact split hypotheses: 941 → 9,41; 965 → 96,5; 934 → 9,34; 336 → 33,6; 649 → 64,9; and 244 → 2,44. For example, splitting 244 would complete futuris. That linguistic fit is not proof of an internal manuscript boundary.

Neither 800 nor 1400 can be fully partitioned into assigned alphabet values. Finding the same numbers in another list does not establish their meanings in this letter. Apparent anomalies such as nonoblkwiscemur and haecsunr are retained, not silently corrected.

Technical checks: what the shuffled controls do—and do not—show

The local evidence package runs 1,000 shuffled-key controls and 1,000 within-line shuffles. The score is agreement at 115 positions in the four originally tested excerpts (safety, the instruction, heirs, and the closing; not the newly illustrated ports excerpt). The proposed output agrees at all 115 positions; mean agreement is 4.506 for key shuffles and 13.747 for within-line shuffles. Each family's maximum is 24.

The reference strings came from this key's own output. These comparisons are retrospective sanity checks, not independent language tests or proof of a correct decipherment. Both controls preserve alphabet coverage. The already-inspected continuation is not a held-out test.

Corrections and limits

An earlier “733/754 verified, about 97% complete” claim was withdrawn. Alphabet coverage is not accuracy. Earlier tentative dictionary substitutions were also not established. No complete translation, expert validation, or priority claim is made. Separate letter-cipher material on the images is outside this result.
